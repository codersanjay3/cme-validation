# Who Absorbs Short-Horizon Selling? A Contrarian Novelty Audit of the Chronotypic Market Ecology Pivots

**Executive summary.** The pivot that looks most novel, horizon contagion through portfolio-overlap networks (Pivot A), is novel largely because it cannot be identified with the data that make it look feasible, and we recommend against building the paper on it. The most defensible pivot is a different paper: a US stress-episode study of liquidity absorption by holder horizon, asking whether long-horizon institutions take the other side of short-horizon selling within the same stock, built on a rebuilt, share-based, unit-consistent 13F panel. That paper extends two published anchors that stop exactly where the question begins: Cella, Ellul and Giannetti show that short-horizon institutions sold about 21% of their holdings around Lehman against 7% for long-horizon institutions, a level result [1], and Chan, Cheng and Hameed conjecture, but never test at the holder level, that "long-horizon funds can provide liquidity to active mutual funds during fire sales" [2]. The competing evidence is real and must be confronted rather than avoided: in the COVID-19 crash retail investors, not long-horizon institutions, were the identified liquidity providers [3], and in distressed liquidations other managers trade in the same direction and prey on the seller [4]. That conflict is what makes the question publishable. The measurement-integrity work (the 2023 SEC dollar-unit break, share-based horizons) is a necessary credibility section, not a headline, because drift-neutral churn has been standard since 2005 [5]. Pivot D is a motivating fact that is mostly mechanical, Pivot E survives only as a disciplined final section, and Pivot C is a footnote plus a correction. Best-fit outlets are JFQA, Review of Finance and Management Science, with Finance Research Letters as the home for a short replication-and-measurement letter.

## 1. Where the Current CME Paper Stands

### 1.1 What the current paper actually shows

The Chronotypic Market Ecology (CME) paper makes three kinds of claims: a simulated mechanism (horizon homophily lowers volatility through a cross-horizon friction channel), an empirical network fact (similar-horizon institutions hold similar portfolios, mean correlation 0.158), and two empirical market-level results (an aggregate horizon-diversity index that tracks VIX, and a stock-level dispersion slope). Every one of those pieces has a problem that a referee at an impact-factor-above-5 journal will find within an afternoon.

The aggregate horizon-diversity result is already dead by the authors' own hand: the r = -0.53 correlation with VIX vanishes to r = -0.06 when turnover is computed from share changes, which means the index was measuring price drift, not horizons. The project's own data add a second, independent defect. The SEC switched reported 13F market values from thousands of dollars to dollars for filings from January 3, 2023, and in the project cache this produces a 1000x jump at 2022Q4; the paper's reported 2022Q4 minimum of the diversity index (1.021) is therefore an artefact, the rescaled value is 1.918, and the value-based correlation with VIX moves from -0.526 to -0.641 (the project's own data). The SEC's own page for the free 13F data sets warns that the data are presented "without change from the as-filed submissions" and that the Commission "cannot guarantee the accuracy of the data sets" [6]. A paper that headlines an aggregate series which was simultaneously drift-contaminated and unit-broken cannot be rescued by a robustness table; the series has to be rebuilt and the original figure withdrawn.

The stock-level slope (lower ownership-weighted dispersion of holders' log tau predicts slightly higher next-quarter volatility, within-stock slope -0.011, t = -2.4) is a replication, not a discovery. Chan, Cheng and Hameed's Internet Appendix Table IA1 already reports quarterly Fama-MacBeth regressions of daily return volatility on lagged horizon dispersion (STDCR, the cross-holder standard deviation of churn scaled by mean churn) with coefficients from -0.034 to -0.057 in most specifications, net of size, book-to-market, institutional ownership, illiquidity and liquidity volatility [7]. The CME estimate has the same sign, a smaller magnitude, a shorter sample, and survivors only. That it agrees with CCH is useful; that it is presented as new is not.

The network fact is the most fragile of all. The 0.158 horizon-proximity correlation is predicted by at least three mechanical channels the paper does not control: index-tracking geometry (long-horizon quasi-indexers all hold near-market portfolios), drift-contaminated tau (institutions that hold the same trending stocks inherit the same spurious turnover), and characteristic-based demand [8]. Type-assortative co-holding is already documented: institutions in ownership-network cliques are most likely to be Bushee-dedicated investors and least likely to be quasi-indexers [9].

The simulated friction channel, finally, has the wrong sign against the data. The model says more horizon diversity raises volatility through friction; CCH's IA1 [7] and the project's own stock-level slope both say more diversity goes with lower volatility. Two independent empirical estimates against one uncalibrated simulation is not a close call.

### 1.2 What transfers and what must be discarded

The contrarian reading of this audit is that very little of the CME paper's empirical content transfers as a contribution, but a great deal transfers as infrastructure. What transfers: the 13F panel, the share-based and value-weight horizon series (which together are the raw material for a measurement-integrity section), the daily prices, the N-PORT bond-fund holdings, and the stock-level dispersion regression as an out-of-sample replication of CCH. What must be discarded: the aggregate diversity-VIX result in its current form, the "network microfoundation for the Fractal Market Hypothesis" framing (it leads referees toward the ABM, which is the weakest part), and the friction-channel claim. The 0.158 homophily coefficient survives only as a conditional, motivating fact after controls.

One fact about targeting deserves stating up front, because it changes the user's framing. Finance Research Letters, the current target, already lists an impact factor of 7.1 [10], above the 4-to-5 threshold. The threshold is therefore not what forces a pivot. What forces it is that the current paper's three empirical results are, respectively, an artefact, a replication and a mechanical correlation. The pivot is about having a contribution, not about clearing an impact-factor bar.

**Committed reading.** The CME paper is not salvageable as an incremental rewrite. It must become a paper with a new estimand, and its existing results become supporting material: the stock-level slope as a replication, the share-based versus value-weight comparison and the unit break as a measurement section, and the homophily coefficient as a conditional motivating fact.

## 2. Literature Map

The map is organised by the seven strands the query names. For each strand the question is the same: what has been done, and what does that leave open for a pivot.

### 2.1 Strand (a): horizon heterogeneity, market stability, the Fractal Market Hypothesis and heterogeneous-horizon ABMs

The Fractal Market Hypothesis (Peters 1994) holds that markets are stable when investors with many different horizons are present, because each horizon's selling can be absorbed by investors whose horizon makes the same information irrelevant. The empirical finance literature has tested the stability half of that claim without using Peters' vocabulary. The cleanest published test is CCH: stocks whose institutional holders have more heterogeneous churn rates are more liquid and have lower liquidity volatility, with identification from the 2003 dividend tax cut, and extreme flow-induced trading has a larger price impact where the investor base is less heterogeneous [2]. Their Internet Appendix extends this to return volatility [7]. The theoretical anchor for the absorption mechanism is Bolton, Santos and Scheinkman's model of outside liquidity, in which short-run investors meet liquidity needs either from cash or by selling assets to long-run investors, the latter being "a more efficient source" [11].

The agent-based tradition is older but thinner. LeBaron's evolutionary market lets the horizon mix change through wealth dynamics, and several FMH-labelled ABMs exist; the nearest network ABM to CME is Oldham (2019), in which investor horizon is a fixed class (memory weight 0.95 versus 0.90) that never adapts, and the advice network rewires on performance toward class-specific "Oracles", not on horizon similarity [12]. No ABM in the material reviewed calibrates a horizon-adaptation rate to an estimated empirical coefficient.

**Committed reading.** The FMH's stability claim is no longer a hypothesis in need of a network microfoundation; it has a published empirical test at JFQA [2]. What the literature has not done is observe the absorption mechanism itself. That gap, not the ABM, is where a pivot should land.

### 2.2 Strand (b): institutional horizons from 13F turnover and their effects

The standard institution-level horizon measure is the Gaspar-Massa-Matos churn rate, whose numerator subtracts the passive price change on prior holdings so that only share changes valued at current prices count [5]. Yan and Zhang use the same construction with the minimum of buys and sells to strip investor cash flows, and show that short-term institutional ownership subsumes the return predictability of total institutional ownership and that short-term institutions' trades forecast returns and earnings surprises [13]. Cremers and Pareek build an alternative holding-period (duration) measure and find that stock-level duration predicts next-quarter idiosyncratic volatility and strengthens momentum and reversal anomalies [14].

The stability literature is overwhelmingly about levels. CEG show that short-horizon institutions sell more in turmoil, that the sales are not entirely due to withdrawals, and that stocks held by short-horizon investors fall more and then reverse; they instrument horizon with the correlation between an investor's past performance and withdrawals [1]. Zhu finds that the negative relation between institutional ownership and return skewness is driven by short-term institutions, while long-term ownership is positively related to skewness [15]. Ben-David, Franzoni, Moussawi and Sedunov show that large institutions are "granular": ownership by large institutions predicts higher volatility and greater crisis fragility, and ownership by high-turnover top institutions has the largest relation to volatility [16]. In COVID-19, Glossner, Matos, Ramelli and Wagner find that stocks with higher pre-crisis institutional ownership did worse, mitigated by longer-horizon, passive and foreign ownership [3]. For China A-shares, Fu, Fang, Yang and Yao find that long-term institutional holding mitigates crash risk by curbing managerial myopia [17].

**Committed reading.** Strand (b) is saturated on levels: short-horizon ownership raises crisis selling, volatility and crash risk, and long-horizon ownership mitigates them. A pivot that adds another level regression adds nothing. The open object is the interaction between holders of different horizons within a stock during stress.

### 2.3 Strand (c): dispersion of a stock's ownership base

This strand is where the CME team's instinct was right and its timing was late. CCH define the dispersion measure, show it predicts liquidity, establish the flow-comovement mechanism (fund flows of a low-churn fund comove 0.80 with other low-churn funds against 0.12 with funds outside the group) and identify with a tax shock [2]. The IA extends the result to volatility [7]. In 2026 a Chinese paper by Chen, Lu and Qin builds a trading-horizon homogeneity index explicitly from CCH's coefficient of variation of holders' churn rates and finds that homogeneity raises left-tail systemic risk in A-shares through liquidity shocks and order imbalance [18]. Greenwood and Thesmar's fragility measure, the owner-weight quadratic form on the covariance of owners' flow shocks, is the benchmark against which any composition measure must be raced: an asset "can be fragile because of concentrated ownership, or because its owners face correlated or volatile liquidity shocks" [19].

Deep inference, stated as such: no source says that horizon dispersion is a proxy for low flow covariance, but CCH's within-tercile flow comovement [2] and Greenwood-Thesmar's definition of fragility as flow covariance [19] jointly imply it. A stock held by a horizon-dispersed base is a stock whose owners' flows are less correlated, so dispersion should predict lower fragility almost by construction. That makes the horse race against fragility a necessary test, and it also tells us what a dispersion paper can and cannot claim: a dispersion effect that does not survive fragility is a restatement of Greenwood-Thesmar.

**Committed reading.** Dispersion as a predictor of liquidity and volatility is published in the US [2, 7] and dispersion as a predictor of tail risk is published for China [18]. A US dispersion-to-crash regression would be a modest increment. The mechanism CCH conjecture, holder-level absorption, is the part nobody has tested.

### 2.4 Strand (d): common ownership, overlap networks, connected stocks, fire sales and fragility

Coval and Stafford show that funds with large outflows cut existing positions, creating price pressure in commonly held stocks that reverses, and that "investors who trade against constrained mutual funds earn highly significant returns for providing liquidity when few others are willing or able" [20]. Greenwood and Thesmar turn ownership structure into a fragility measure [19]. Gualdi, Cimini, Primicerio, Di Clemente and Challet build a statistically validated 13F overlap network against a bipartite configuration model null, in which "institutions randomly connect to securities, but the degrees of both institutions and securities are constrained on average to their observed values", and find that validated overlaps rose before the 2007-08 crisis and peaked at it [21]. Blocher shows that mutual funds linked by common holdings transmit flows through return-chasing investors, with a network externality 32-92% as large as lagged own flows [22].

The common-ownership competition literature matters here for one reason only: it has stress-tested the asset-manager-merger shock that a horizon-contagion design would want to borrow, and the shock did not survive. Lewellen and Lowry show that although the merger sample has over 60 events, the treated firms are dominated by BlackRock-BGI in 2009, which coincided with the post-crisis period and tilted toward growth firms; once corrected, they find no evidence that the mergers changed firm decisions [23]. Massa, Schumacher and Wang show that after the BlackRock-BGI announcement, open-end funds with large exposure to commonly held stocks began avoiding those stocks, leading to a permanent change in ownership composition [24]. Gilje, Gormley and Levit compute that 39% to 56% of stock pairs would see a decline, not an increase, in managers' incentives after the same merger [25]. Gerardi, Lowry and Schenone's 2023 review concludes that across the most credible designs there is "relatively little evidence that common ownership causes lower competition" [26]. Ozdagli and Ryfe use staggered changes in shared external asset managers among life insurers and find more similar portfolios and trades for insurers sharing a manager [27, 28].

**Committed reading.** Strand (d) supplies the fire-sale and fragility machinery that an absorption paper needs (Coval-Stafford flow-induced trading, Greenwood-Thesmar fragility, BiCM-validated overlap) and it supplies the strongest warning against a merger-shock design for horizon contagion: mergers move neighbours' holdings directly [24], so they cannot be an excluded shock to neighbours' horizons.

### 2.5 Strand (e): peer effects, herding and contagion among institutions, and identification

Institutional peer effects are well established for what is held and traded. Hong, Kubik and Stein find that a fund manager is more likely to hold, buy or sell a stock when other managers in the same city do [29]. Pool, Stoffman and Yonker find that socially connected (same-neighbourhood) managers have more similar holdings and trades [30]. Blocher finds flow spillovers through overlap networks [22]. Cohen, Lu and Nguyen show that 71% of mutual fund managers' trade directions can be predicted from their own past trading history [31]. None of these studies uses turnover, trading frequency or horizon as the outcome; an audit across roughly 18 search framings found peer-effect papers with trading activity as the outcome only for retail investors [32].

The identification literature is where Pivot A's prospects are decided, and it is unforgiving. Angrist shows that outcome-on-outcome peer regressions are "vacuous, either unity or, if the average is defined as leave-out, determined by a generic intraclass correlation coefficient", and that the designs that manipulate peer characteristics have "uncovered little in the way of socially significant causal effects" [33]. Caeyers and Fafchamps show that excluding the focal unit from its own peer pool creates a mechanical negative correlation that biases peer estimates downward, including with self-selected peers [34]. Chandrasekhar and Lewis prove (their Proposition 3.4) that 2SLS with friends-of-friends instruments built from a sampled graph generically yields an invalid exclusion restriction [35]. Canen and Chadha show that friends-of-friends instruments "may become degenerate" in very sparse networks and "may still provide too little first-stage information" in denser ones [36]. Pastor, Stambaugh and Taylor document the confound that matters most: a panel regression of fund turnover on the leave-one-out average of other funds' turnover, with fund fixed effects, has slope 0.65 (t = 8.65), and the commonality strengthens further with same-category averages [37]; average turnover fluctuates between 59% and 102% a year and has a 95% correlation with the first principal component of fund turnover [38].

**Committed reading.** The toolkit for a peer-effect study exists, but its own authors spent the last decade explaining why the cheap version of it does not identify anything. A horizon-contagion paper would walk into the exact configuration (outcome-on-outcome, sampled network, strong common factor) that this literature was written to reject.

### 2.6 Strand (f): measuring horizons from holdings and price-drift contamination

The CME finding that value-weight turnover is contaminated by price drift is correct and already known. Gaspar, Massa and Matos' churn numerator removes passive price changes by construction [5]; Yan and Zhang use the same numerator [13]; Zhu uses it [15]; CCH use it and report a share-based turnover robustness check with similar results [2]. Tucker documents a "Modified Turnover" measure from the mutual-fund literature that compares observed weights with buy-and-hold weights to capture "the active changes by a mutual fund manager rather than capturing mechanical portfolio adjustments driven by stock price fluctuations" [39]. Cremers and Pareek's duration measure, built from holding periods, sidesteps the issue [14].

The 2023 unit break is a different and less-discussed measurement problem. The SEC's free data sets cover all filers from mid-2013 [6], and a reported-value field that changes units mid-sample will silently corrupt any series that concatenates reported values across quarters (the project's own data show the 1000x jump at 2022Q4). Deep inference: churn-type measures built from share counts and prices, as in [5], are immune to the break because they never use the reported value field; measures built from reported values across the break, including the CME value-weight tau and diversity index, are not. The defect is therefore specific to a class of measures, which is exactly why it is worth documenting and why it is not a stand-alone contribution.

**Committed reading.** Pivot C is pre-empted as a methodological claim. Its residual value is as a documented, replicable measurement section that any paper using free SEC 13F data across 2023 must contain.

### 2.7 Strand (g): homophily in investor networks

Homophily in co-holding is documented as type assortativity: clique members are disproportionately dedicated investors, and within-clique dispersion of institution type is smaller than across the sample [9]. CCH document horizon-sorted flow comovement [2]. Koijen and Yogo's characteristics-based demand system implies that institutions whose demand loads similarly on characteristics will co-hold mechanically [40]. Ozdagli and Ryfe show that a shared asset manager produces similar portfolios [28]. No paper reviewed regresses pairwise portfolio similarity on pairwise horizon proximity with the controls that would make the result non-mechanical [8].

**Committed reading.** Horizon homophily is unsurprising and largely mechanical as measured. It is a motivating fact for any network paper, not a contribution by itself.

### 3.1 Pivot A: horizon contagion is a trap

**The strongest case for A.** The steelman deserves its full weight. An audit across roughly 18 framings found no paper in which an institution's turnover, holding period or horizon is the outcome of a network peer effect [32]. Institutional peer-effect papers target holdings, trades and flows [29, 30, 22]; turnover peer effects exist only for retail. The project's network is structurally suitable for friends-of-friends instruments: about 100 institutions per quarter, median pairwise cosine 0.03-0.07, and every node has a median of 32-42 friends-of-friends who are not friends (the project's own data). The variance decomposition is encouraging: within-institution sd of log tau is 0.55 against 0.92 in total, AR(1) is 0.75 (the project's own data), which implies a within-institution variance share of about 36% and a shock half-life near 2.4 quarters [41]; after institution and quarter fixed effects 33% of the variance of log tau remains, while quarter effects alone explain only about 2% (the project's own data). That last number matters, because it means the market-wide common turnover factor that Pastor, Stambaugh and Taylor document for mutual funds [38] is small in this panel. The free SEC data sets cover every filer from 2013 [6], so the sampled-network critique can be answered without WRDS [42].

**Why it is still a trap.** Four objections, each of which a referee can raise in a sentence and the authors would need a paper's worth of work to answer.

*First, the common component is style-level, and the proposed exclusion does not touch it.* The 2% market-wide share shows that the threat is not the market factor; it is the style factor. PST show that adding same-category average turnover raises the explanatory power of the commonality regression and that "commonality in turnover is especially high among funds sharing similar characteristics" [37]. CCH show that flows comove 0.80 within a low-churn group against 0.12 outside it [2]. Deep inference: PST's style-level commonality [37] and the fact that overlap-network neighbours are style neighbours by construction jointly imply that a neighbour's horizon and the focal institution's horizon load on a shared style-specific, time-varying factor. Computing the neighbour's horizon only from stocks the focal institution does not hold removes stock-level and event-level channels (an index deletion or a cash-out merger in a co-held name), but it cannot remove a strategy-level opportunity set or a style-level flow shock, because those move turnover in every stock the style trades.

*Second, the signal the paper is looking for is tiny relative to the noise it must be separated from.* This is an inference from the project's own numbers, not a sourced finding, so the chain is stated explicitly. Total variance of log tau is 0.92 squared, about 0.85; after fixed effects 33% remains, about 0.28. The model's assumed adaptation rate is 0.06 per quarter. Even if the gap between neighbours' and own horizon had a variance as large as the total cross-sectional variance, a 0.06 peer response would generate quarterly variation of roughly 0.06 squared times 0.85, about 0.003, or about 1% of the residual variance. The implication is not that the estimate is underpowered; with thousands of institution-quarters it may be statistically detectable. The implication is that any correlated-effects leak worth about 1% of residual variance, a style-quarter shock with heterogeneous loadings, a persistent flow factor, a filer reorganisation, would reproduce the entire estimated effect. No design built on observational overlap neighbours can rule out a leak that small.

*Third, persistence makes the dynamic panel biased by an amount of the same order as the parameter.* With institution fixed effects, a lagged dependent variable and roughly 50 quarters, the standard Nickell approximation puts the bias on the persistence coefficient near (1 + 0.75)/(50 - 1), about 0.036 (inference from the project's AR(1) of 0.75 and its 50 quarters, using the textbook formula, not a sourced result). Because horizon homophily makes the neighbour average correlated with the focal institution's own lag, part of that bias loads onto the peer coefficient. A bias of about 0.036 is more than half of the 0.06 the paper would be trying to estimate. Caeyers and Fafchamps add the opposite-signed exclusion bias from leave-out means [34], so the sign of the net bias is unknown. A referee will say the estimate is uninterpretable in either direction, and the authors will not be able to prove otherwise without simulation-based bias correction that itself depends on the correlated-effects structure.

*Fourth, the friends-of-friends instrument is invalid in exactly the network the team has.* The top-150 filer panel is a sample of a population of thousands of filers, and Chandrasekhar and Lewis prove that friends-of-friends instruments built from a sampled graph generically violate exclusion [35]. The project's own diagnostic contains a second version of the same problem that has not been noticed. Deep inference: the network is a top-5 nearest-neighbour graph, and widening it to top-10 raises the median friends-of-friends count from 32-42 to about 60-70 (the project's own diagnostics), which means many "friends-of-friends who are not friends" are simply the sixth- to tenth-nearest neighbours, that is, real peers excluded by an arbitrary threshold. Chandrasekhar and Lewis's mechanism [35] applies to threshold truncation as much as to node sampling: a peer omitted from the observed graph is a channel through which the instrument enters the outcome. Moving to the full universe answers node sampling but not threshold truncation, and it pushes the network toward the density regime where Canen and Chadha show the instruments carry little first-stage information [36].

**The shock design does not rescue it either.** The identification literature's answer is to replace instruments with exogenous shocks to neighbours [43]. The obvious shock, asset-manager mergers, has been discredited for exactly this kind of use: the merger sample is dominated by BlackRock-BGI in 2009 and tilted toward growth firms [23], mergers directly change other funds' holdings of commonly held stocks [24], which violates exclusion for a horizon test because neighbours respond to concentration rather than to the merging party's horizon, and the treatment intensity is ambiguous in sign [25]. Manager-level shocks are the remaining option. Ozdagli and Ryfe's staggered shared-manager changes [27] show that manager-level variation is usable in institutional data, and manager deaths or departures are a plausible next step, but nobody has shown that a 13F filer of the size in the top-150 panel changes its turnover measurably when one portfolio manager leaves, and nobody has counted how many such events exist in 2013-2026. Trait persistence makes this harder, not easier: CEG describe the churn ratio as "very stable for institutions across time" [1], and 71% of managers' trade directions are predictable from their own history [31]. A paper whose identifying events are rare and whose outcome barely moves is a paper that can produce a null, and a null in a reflection-plus-exclusion setting is uninformative [34, 33].

**Verdict on A.** Novel as an estimand, with perhaps 70% confidence that no near-duplicate exists [32], and therefore genuinely tempting. But the novelty and the identification problem are the same fact seen from two sides: the question is open because the obvious design does not answer it. We put the probability that a top-150 or full-universe 13F version of A clears a JFQA or RFS referee on identification at well under one half. The defensible increment, if the team insists, is a narrowly worded descriptive result ("horizon comovement among overlap neighbours, bounded above by an Oster-type or partial-identification calculation, after style-by-quarter effects"), not a causal contagion claim. What would change this verdict: 30 or more clean manager-level shocks with measurable neighbour-horizon changes and no pre-trends, plus a peer coefficient that survives style-by-quarter fixed effects and is zero for future neighbours [43].

### 3.2 Pivot B: holder-horizon dispersion and within-stock absorption

**Already done?** The dispersion half, mostly yes. CCH publish the measure, the liquidity results, the mechanism, the tax-shock identification and the Coval-Stafford price-impact interaction [2], and the volatility result appears in their IA [7]. Chen, Lu and Qin publish dispersion-to-tail-risk for China in 2026 [18]. The absorption half, no. CCH's absorption mechanism is argued, not observed: they conjecture that long-horizon funds can supply liquidity in fire sales but test only stock-level price impact [2]. CEG show relative selling by horizon class around Lehman, a level contrast [1]. Glossner et al. ask who provided liquidity in 2020 and find retail, not institutions, and they note that downscaling institutions "did not buy stocks abandoned in Q1 by their fellow downscaling institutional investors" [3], but they do not split counterparties by horizon within a stock. Timmer finds that insurers and pension funds buy debt securities when prices fall, while banks and investment funds trade procyclically, but by institution type, in German bonds [44].

**Closest papers.** CCH [2], CEG [1], Glossner et al. [3], Coval-Stafford [20], Greenwood-Thesmar [19], Chen-Lu-Qin [18].

**Novel increment.** A holder-by-stock test in US stress episodes of whether long-horizon co-holders increase their positions more in stocks where short-horizon co-holders face larger flow-induced selling, with the stock-level consequence (price drop and reversal) raced against Greenwood-Thesmar fragility and against short-horizon ownership levels. The contrarian point is that the literature's evidence on this question genuinely conflicts. Theory says long-horizon investors are the efficient outside liquidity [11], Coval-Stafford say liquidity providers to fire sellers earn high returns [20], Timmer says long-horizon types are countercyclical [44]. Against that, Glossner et al. say retail absorbed and institutions did not buy back [3], CEG say long-horizon holders also sold [1], and Barbon, Di Maggio, Franzoni and Landier show that informed managers prey on liquidating funds, earning 45 to 70 basis points in the few days of the fire sale [4]. A paper that adjudicates this conflict with a design that compares the same long-horizon holder across stocks with different short-horizon selling pressure is a paper with a question a referee cannot dismiss as already answered.

**Verdict on B.** Dispersion alone: a section. Absorption: the recommended core. We hold this with moderate confidence; the main residual risk is an unseen 2024-2026 working paper on counterparty-level absorption.

### 3.3 Pivot C: price-drift contamination

**Already done?** Yes. Drift-neutral churn has been the standard since Gaspar, Massa and Matos [5], Yan and Zhang use it [13], CCH report share-based robustness [2], and the mutual-fund literature has a Modified Turnover measure designed for exactly this purpose [39].

**Novel increment.** Two narrow items: documenting that the value-weight construction reverses a headline result (r = -0.53 to -0.06), and documenting the 2023 unit break and its consequences for any reported-value series built from the free SEC data (the project's own data; SEC data-set notes [6]). These are valuable for replicability but would not carry a paper at the target tier. Finance Research Letters says it especially welcomes papers that "shed insight on the replicability or lack thereof of established results" [10], which makes it the one outlet where a short measurement-and-replication letter could stand on its own.

**Verdict on C.** Footnote and correction in the main paper; optionally a separate FRL letter.

### 3.4 Pivot D: horizon homophily in co-holding

**Already done?** Partially. Type assortativity in co-holding cliques [9] and horizon-sorted flow comovement [2] are published. The exact pairwise, continuous-horizon design is not.

**Novel increment.** A residual fact: horizon proximity predicts similarity on active weights (relative to the market portfolio), with share-based tau, institution fixed effects in a dyadic regression, controls for size distance, number of positions, same Bushee type and same parent, and excess BiCM-validated links for horizon-proximate pairs [21, 8]. Koijen-Yogo latent-demand residuals [40] would be the strongest version. The t = 19 must be recomputed with dyadic-robust inference, because the 50 quarterly correlations share nodes and portfolios and are closer to one persistent structural fact observed 50 times than to 50 confirmations [8].

**Verdict on D.** A motivating fact that would appear in Section 2 of the absorption paper (horizon classes are not random partitions of the market; they hold overlapping books), not a contribution.

### 3.5 Pivot E: calibrated ABM with endogenous horizon adaptation

**Already done?** Not in this form. Oldham's network ABM has fixed horizon classes and performance-based rewiring and is uncalibrated [12].

**Novel increment, and its dependency.** E's novelty depends on calibrating the adaptation rate to an estimated peer coefficient, which means E inherits every identification problem of A. The contrarian alternative is to give the model a different calibration target: an absorption elasticity estimated in Pivot B, which is identified with much stronger tools. A model whose liquidity-provision channel is disciplined by an estimated within-stock absorption coefficient, and which is then tested on a held-out stress episode, would be novel relative to Oldham [12] and would reverse the current model's contradicted friction sign rather than defend it. The best topical home for ABMs, the Journal of Economic Dynamics and Control, lists an impact factor of 1.8 [45], below the user's threshold, so E cannot be the headline.

**Verdict on E.** A supporting final section of the absorption paper, or nothing.

## 4. Recommended Pivot

### 4.1 The paper

**Working title:** "Who Absorbs Short-Horizon Selling? Holder Horizons and Within-Stock Liquidity Provision in US Stress Episodes."

**Core question.** In stress episodes, when short-horizon institutions sell a stock, do long-horizon institutions that already hold the same stock buy, sell less than composition predicts, or sell alongside them; and does the answer explain which stocks fall more and recover faster?

**Three contributions, in order of weight.**

1. *A counterparty-level test of the absorption mechanism.* CCH's mechanism [2], Bolton-Santos-Scheinkman's outside liquidity [11] and the FMH's informal claim all predict that long-horizon holders absorb; Glossner et al. [3] and Barbon et al. [4] point the other way. The paper estimates, within holder and within stock, how a long-horizon holder's position change responds to the flow-induced selling of its short-horizon co-holders in the same stock. The likely answer, given the conflicting evidence, is relative rather than absolute absorption: long-horizon holders sell less where short-horizon pressure is high, without becoming net buyers [46]. That is still a first-order fact, because it locates where the FMH's stabilising force operates and where it fails, and the residual (what 13F filers as a group do not absorb) measures non-filer absorption.

2. *A stock-level horse race.* Does horizon dispersion predict drawdowns and reversals in stress episodes beyond Greenwood-Thesmar fragility [19], short-horizon ownership levels [1], long-term and passive ownership [3] and large-institution granularity [16]? CME's stock-level slope enters here as an out-of-sample replication of CCH IA1 [7].

3. *A measurement-integrity section.* The rebuilt full-universe, share-based panel, with the 2023 unit break documented and the value-weight versus share-based comparison reported, is published as part of the paper and as a replication package.

An optional fourth section calibrates a small model's liquidity-provision channel to the absorption estimate (Section 3.5), replacing the friction channel.

### 4.2 Why this, and not horizon contagion

The argument is not that A is uninteresting; it is that B-reframed dominates on every dimension a referee scores. On identification, the absorption test borrows the strongest instrument in this literature, Coval-Stafford flow-induced selling [20], already used by CCH in this exact context [2], and CEG's funding-fragility instrument for horizon [1], instead of friends-of-friends instruments that are invalid in sampled networks [35]. On novelty, it has a named, published, conflicting pair of findings to adjudicate [3, 1, 4, 44], which is the easiest kind of contribution to sell in an introduction. On feasibility, it needs the data the team has or can get free (the full 13F universe from the SEC [6]) plus CRSP, which the team has already requested. On downside protection, every outcome is publishable: absorption, relative absorption or co-selling are all informative, whereas a null in A is uninterpretable [34].

There is also a strategic reason. The pivot keeps the CME team's intellectual identity, horizon heterogeneity as a source of stability, and replaces an unobservable mechanism (horizon adaptation through networks) with an observable one (position changes by horizon class within a stock during stress). The FMH framing survives in one sentence of the introduction rather than as the paper's spine.

### 4.3 What would make us change the recommendation

The contrarian view should be falsifiable. We would move back toward A if (i) the team counts 30 or more manager-level shocks among 13F filers in 2013-2026 with measurable horizon effects and no pre-trends, and (ii) a pilot regression on the full universe shows a neighbour-horizon coefficient that survives style-by-quarter fixed effects and is zero for future neighbours [43]. We would downgrade B if a 2024-2026 working paper already runs a holder-by-stock absorption test by horizon in US stress episodes. Neither condition can be checked from the literature alone; both are cheap to check with the team's data or a targeted SSRN search.

## 6. Identification Strategy and Data Upgrades

### 6.1 Step zero: rebuild the panel

Every result in the current paper inherits two defects: a top-150 sample and value-based series that cross the 2023 unit break. The rebuild is a precondition, not a contribution.

1. **Full universe from the free SEC 13F data sets**, which cover all filers from July 2013 and include share counts [6]. Share counts make the share-based (drift-neutral) horizon and the Gaspar-Massa-Matos churn [5] computable directly, and the full universe removes the top-150 sampling problem for any network-based control.
2. **Rescale or avoid reported values.** Any series using the reported value field across January 2023 must be rescaled by filing date (the project's own data); share-based measures avoid the issue.
3. **CRSP via WRDS** for prices, shares outstanding, returns and delisted firms. The current price data cover mapped survivors only, which is a fatal flaw for a stress-episode paper: the stocks most likely to delist after a crash are exactly those whose absorption failed.
4. **Pre-2013 holdings from WRDS (Thomson s34)** if the 2008 episode is to be included, which it should be for an RFS-level submission because it is CEG's central episode [1].
5. **Fund flows** (CRSP Mutual Fund database) to build Coval-Stafford flow-induced trading [20] for the short-horizon sellers.

### 6.2 Defining horizon and stress

**Horizon.** Classify each institution by its predetermined, pre-episode average share-based churn, following CEG's use of a lagged average churn ratio [1] and Yan-Zhang's tercile classification [13]. Report results with GMM churn [5] and with a duration measure [14] as alternatives. Never use value-weight turnover.

**Stress episodes.** Adopt CEG's turmoil definition (quarters with a month in which S&P 500 returns fall in the bottom 5th percentile and VIX changes exceed the 95th percentile) [1], applied to 2013-2026, plus 2008 when WRDS data arrive. Which post-2013 quarters qualify must be computed, not assumed; COVID-19's Q1-2020 is the obvious candidate and connects the paper directly to Glossner et al. [3].

### 6.3 The core design

**Holder-by-stock absorption regression.** For holder h of stock i in episode e, the outcome is the change in shares held as a fraction of shares outstanding. The key regressor is the flow-induced selling pressure from short-horizon co-holders of stock i, interacted with h's horizon class. Fixed effects: holder-by-episode (absorbs each holder's own funding shock, redemptions and aggregate risk appetite) and stock-by-episode (absorbs news, fundamentals and the stock's aggregate demand). The coefficient of interest is identified by comparing the same long-horizon holder across stocks that face different short-horizon flow-induced pressure in the same episode. This is the within-holder, across-stock logic that the literature uses to separate credit supply from demand, applied to liquidity provision; we know of no paper that applies it to absorption by horizon, and its fixed-effect structure answers the most obvious referee objection (that long-horizon holders simply had more cash).

Deep inference on what the design can and cannot see: quarterly 13F holdings record net changes only, so intra-quarter round trips are invisible [46]; and because every share sold by a filer is bought by someone, the aggregate change in 13F-reported holdings of a stock equals minus the change held by non-filers (retail, small institutions, foreign non-filers). The paper can therefore decompose absorption into long-horizon filers, other filers and non-filers by accounting identity, which connects the 13F result to Glossner et al.'s retail finding [3] without needing retail data.

**Instruments.** Short-horizon co-holders' selling is endogenous to news about stock i. Instrument it with Coval-Stafford flow-induced selling, the pro-rata liquidation implied by outflows from short-horizon funds [20], as CCH do [2]. Instrument holder horizon itself with CEG's funding-fragility measure, the pre-period correlation between past performance and withdrawals [1].

**Stock-level consequence.** Regress the episode drawdown and subsequent reversal on pre-episode horizon dispersion, short-horizon ownership, Greenwood-Thesmar fragility [19], long-term and passive ownership [3] and large-institution ownership [16], with industry-by-episode fixed effects, then add the estimated holder-level absorption capacity of the stock's long-horizon base. Report the dispersion slope beside CCH IA1 [7] as a replication.

**Placebos.** Non-stress quarters (absorption should be weaker or absent); flow-induced selling in stocks the long-horizon holder does not hold (should have no effect on its position in stock i); pre-episode trends in long-horizon positions.

**Inference.** With few episodes, cluster by stock and report episode-by-episode estimates; treat the pooled estimate as a summary, not the identifying variation.

### 6.4 Extensions that use existing data

The N-PORT bond-fund holdings allow an out-of-asset-class test in March 2020 corporate bonds, where Timmer's type-level countercyclicality of insurers and pension funds [44] and Ozdagli and Ryfe's shared-manager similarity among insurers [28] provide the comparison. The horizon-homophily fact (Pivot D) enters as a descriptive section after BiCM validation [21].

## 7. Referee Objections the Pivot Must Survive

1. **"This is CCH or CEG again."** Answer: CCH test stock-level liquidity and price impact and only conjecture holder-level absorption [2]; CEG compare selling levels by horizon class [1]. Neither estimates how a given long-horizon holder responds to short-horizon co-holders' pressure in the same stock. The introduction must show this side by side, in a table, in the first three pages.

2. **"Glossner et al. already showed retail absorbed, not institutions."** Answer: their result is aggregate and for one episode [3]; ours is relative and within stock, across several episodes. The accounting decomposition into long-horizon filers, other filers and non-filers nests their finding rather than contradicting it. If long-horizon filers do not absorb, that is a publishable confirmation of Glossner et al. across episodes, with the horizon dimension added.

3. **"Horizon is endogenous to the stress."** Answer: horizons are predetermined, measured before the episode, and instrumented with funding fragility [1]. Trait persistence, so damaging to Pivot A, helps here: a stable horizon is a credible predetermined characteristic [1, 31].

4. **"Long-horizon means passive, and passive buying is mechanical index rebalancing."** Answer: separate passive from active long-horizon holders, as Glossner et al. distinguish passive and long-term ownership [3]; exclude index-reconstitution quarters; show the result for active long-horizon holders alone.

5. **"Quarterly data cannot see absorption."** Answer: concede intra-quarter invisibility [46], define absorption as net, and bound it. Where possible, use higher-frequency fund-level holdings for the registered-fund subset to show the quarterly result is not an artefact of netting.

6. **"Survivors only."** Answer: CRSP with delisting returns is a condition of submission, not a robustness check. Without it the paper should not go out.

7. **"Your horizon measure is contaminated."** Answer: share-based churn [5], with value-weight shown only to document the contamination, and with the 2023 unit break documented [6]. This is where the measurement section pays for itself: it pre-empts the objection that destroyed the CME paper's aggregate result.

8. **"Dispersion is just Greenwood-Thesmar fragility."** Answer: race them [19]. If dispersion loses, report it: the holder-level absorption result does not depend on dispersion winning, and showing that dispersion works through flow covariance is itself a clarification of CCH [2].

9. **"Flow-induced trading instruments are themselves contested."** Answer: report results with and without the instrument, with Coval-Stafford's own pro-rata construction [20], and with episode-specific placebo flows.

10. **"Why not horizon contagion? It is the more novel question."** Answer, and this is the contrarian thesis in one paragraph: the question is open because overlap-network peer designs do not identify it. The common component is style-level [37], the network is sampled and threshold-truncated [35], friends-of-friends instruments are weak or invalid [36], merger shocks violate exclusion [24, 23], and the implied effect is small relative to residual variance. A paper that asks a less novel question and answers it credibly will publish at JFQA or Review of Finance; a paper that asks the novel question and cannot answer it will not.

**Looking forward.** The free SEC data sets have made full-universe 13F research cheap [6], and the next few years will likely bring more papers on who provides liquidity in stress, in part because COVID-19 and the 2022 rate shock gave the field new episodes with retail data [3]. A team that publishes the first holder-by-stock absorption-by-horizon test, with a clean free-data build, positions itself to own the follow-ups: bond-fund absorption in N-PORT data, cross-country versions following Chen, Lu and Qin's Chinese evidence [18], and, once a credible shock is found, the horizon-contagion paper that is premature today. Horizon contagion is a good second paper. It is a bad first one.
