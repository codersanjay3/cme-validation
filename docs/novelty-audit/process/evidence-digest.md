### Atomic item: Q1a Horizon heterogeneity, FMH and heterogeneous-horizon ABMs
- Investors endogenously specialize in trading frequency in a noisy REE; high- and low-frequency investors coexist and trade with each other. (theoretical)
  > We show that high- and low-frequency investors coexist, trade with each other, and make money from each other.
  [a-model-of-multi-frequency-trade]
- Restricting high-frequency trading affects high-frequency price efficiency but leaves low-frequency efficiency unaffected. (theoretical)
  > a restriction on high-frequency trading affects efficiency of prices at high frequencies, but leaves low-frequency efficiency unaffected
  [a-model-of-multi-frequency-trade]
- In LeBaron's ABM, horizon distribution evolves endogenously via wealth-based replacement; mixed short/long horizon populations raise return variability. (theoretical)
  > populations with both short- and long-horizon agents increase return variability, and leave patterns in volatility and trading volume similar to actual financial markets
  [econpapers-evolution-and-time-horizons-in-an-agent-based-stock-market]
- FMH predictions about horizon-specific trading activity fit DJI, NASDAQ and S&P500 behaviour during the GFC. (empirical)
  > predictions of fractal markets hypothesis actually fit the observed behavior quite well
  [fractal-markets-hypothesis-and-the-global-financial-crisis]
- Parsa estimates an investor-specific fixed effect in trading frequency; securities held by high-frequency investors have higher volatility, driven by cash-flow component; lowest-frequency quintile earns ~6pp/yr more. (empirical)
  > identifies an investor-specific fixed effect in the frequency of trading
  [investors-horizon-and-stock-prices]
- Trades of very large institutions (Pareto size exponent ~1) in illiquid markets generate return and volume power laws (exponents ~3 and ~1.5) without news; heterogeneity is in size, not horizon. (theoretical)
  > We present a theory of excess stock market volatility, in which market movements are due to trades by very large institutional investors in relatively illiquid markets.
  [nber-working-paper-series-3]
- Peer effect is on stock choice (holdings and trades); no turnover, frequency or horizon outcome is studied. (empirical)
  > These results can be interpreted in terms of an epidemic model in which investors spread information about stocks to one another by word of mouth.
  [nber-working-paper-series]
- With heterogeneous investor horizons and stochastic transaction costs, short-horizon investors hold only liquid assets and illiquid-asset liquidity premia are determined by horizon heterogeneity and liquid/illiquid correlation. (theoretical)
  > Short-term investors hold only liquid assets in equilibrium. This generates segmentation effects in the pricing of liquid versus illiquid assets.
  [pricing-liquidity-risk-with-heterogeneous-investment]
- Issuers funded by similar investors face higher funding-liquidity risk because loss of funding from similar investors cannot be substituted in a crisis. (empirical)
  > The issuer cannot substitute the loss of funding from similar investors in a crisis.
  [similar-investors1]

### Atomic item: Q1b Institutional horizons from 13F and their effects
- The paper builds a portfolio-similarity network but does not use investment horizon or turnover as an attribute or determinant of similarity. (empirical)
  > we model the market in a similarity network, by considering investors as nodes, connected with links representing portfolio similarity
  [arxiv14022494v2-q-fingn-28-aug-2014]
- Hedge-fund managers sharing a prior employer (more so with overlapping tenure) invest more similarly; closest social-tie convergence paper for funds, but on returns/holdings, not turnover or horizon. (empirical)
  > If they overlapped in employment, increasing the chance of social ties, investments become even more similar. The joint effect accounts for up to two thirds of the difference in investing behavior.
  [birds-of-a-feather-do-hedge-fund-managers-flock-together]
- Negative association between institutional ownership and return skewness is driven by short-term institutional investors; long-term institutional ownership is positively related to skewness. (empirical)
  > this inverse relation is actually driven by short-term institutional investors. Moreover, we document that, in contrast to short-term institutional investors' trading, long-term institutional investors' holdings and trading are positively related to skewness.
  [crash-risk-and-investor-horizon]
- Churn rate of Yan-Zhang (2009) subtracts price change on prior holdings from buys and sells, so share-based churn is price-drift corrected; investors ranked into tertiles of 4-quarter average churn. (theoretical)
  > CR_sell = sum |N_{j,i,t} P_{i,t} - N_{j,i,t-1} P_{i,t-1} - N_{j,i,t-1} dP_{i,t}|; short-term = top tertile of AVG_CR, long-term = bottom tertile
  [crash-risk-and-investor-horizon]
- In the COVID-19 crash, stocks with higher pre-crisis institutional ownership did worse, mitigated by long-term, passive and foreign IO. (empirical)
  > it is mitigated by the presence of longer-horizon, passive, and foreign institutional investors.
  [do-institutional-investors-stabilize]
- Retail investors, not long-horizon institutions, are the identified liquidity providers opposite institutional selling in the crash; hedge funds sold indiscriminately. (empirical)
  > At least some retail investors (e.g., Robinhood investors) appear to have provided liquidity.
  [do-institutional-investors-stabilize]
- Average stock duration (holding-time-based) predicts next-quarter idiosyncratic volatility and strengthens momentum/reversal anomalies. (empirical)
  > average stock duration can indeed explain next quarter's stock idiosyncratic volatility after controlling for lagged volatility and other stock characteristics
  [electronic-copy-available-at-httpssrncomabstract1464954]
- Friends-of-friends IVs degenerate in very sparse networks and are weak in denser ones; AR inference and adjacency scaling are remedies. (theoretical)
  > in extremely sparse networks, friends-of-friends instruments may become degenerate, while in denser networks they may still provide too little first-stage information.
  [empirical-challenges-with-peers-of-peers-instruments]
- Ownership by low-churn and top-5 institutions lowers stock price delay; ownership by high-churn, concentrated and skilled institutions raises it. (empirical)
  > ownership from top 5 and low churn rate institutions helps lower price delay while high churn rate, concentrated, and skilled institutional ownership increases the delay.
  [heterogeneity-of-institutional-ownership-and-stock-price-delay]
- Churn rate is min(aggregate buys, aggregate sells) over average holdings value, with price change on prior holdings netted out, averaged over four quarters and split into terciles; heterogeneity is operationalized as ownership share of tercile buckets, not within-stock dispersion. (theoretical)
  > we calculate each institution's average churn rate over the past four quarters ... lowest tercile are classified as low churn rate institutions while those in the highest tercile are considered as high churn rate institutions.
  [heterogeneity-of-institutional-ownership-and-stock-price-delay]
- Short-horizon institutions drive the positive ownership-valuation link globally (34 countries), stronger where stock liquidity is high. (empirical)
  > The positive association between institutional ownership and firm value is driven by short-horizon institutions.
  [institutional-investment-horizon]
- Yan-Zhang use churn rate with min(buy,sell) over average value and price-adjusted numerator; short-term IO subsumes total IO return predictability. (empirical)
  > CR_k,t = min(CR buy, CR sell)/average of S_t P_t + S_{t-1}P_{t-1} over 2
  [institutional-investors-and-equity-returns]
- Flows are more correlated among funds in the same churn-rate tercile, i.e. horizon-proximate funds share liquidity demand; evidence is on flows not holdings similarity. (empirical)
  > The comovement in fund flows among funds with similar investment horizons (i.e., within the same CR tercile)
  [investor-heterogeneity-and-liquidity]
- Long-term investor ownership increases investment and equity issuance and lowers payouts when firms are undervalued. (empirical)
  > for undervalued firms, investment and equity financing are increasing in investor horizons and payouts are decreasing in investor horizons
  [investor-horizons-and-corporate-policies]
- Short-term (high-churn) institutions sold ~21% of portfolios around Lehman vs 7% for long-term; stocks held by short-term investors fell more and reversed more. (empirical)
  > short-term investors sold on average 21% of their portfolios, while long-tern investors sold only 7%
  [investors-horizons-and-the-amplification-of-market-shocks]
- Short-horizon institutions (by lagged-average churn ratio) sell more than long-horizon institutions in market-turmoil episodes, amplifying price drops and reversals. (empirical)
  > short horizon investors sell significantly more than long horizon investors during periods of market turmoil and that these sales are not entirely due to withdrawals
  [issn-0956-8549-717-2]
- Horizon is instrumented with the correlation between past performance and withdrawals (funding-fragility instrument), a template for identification. (empirical)
  > Using this correlation as an instrument for investor turnover, we exploit the variation in shareholder horizon that is less likely to be driven by stocks' unobserved heterogeneity
  [issn-0956-8549-717-2]
- Gaspar-Massa-Matos churn rate nets out passive price changes by valuing share changes at current price, so price drift is removed from the numerator. (empirical)
  > CR = sum |N_t P_t - N_{t-1} P_{t-1} - N_{t-1} dP| / sum (N_t P_t + N_{t-1} P_{t-1})/2; range of the churn rate is [0, 2]
  [journal-of-financial-economics-76-2005-135165]
- Regressing an outcome on its own group mean is vacuous: coefficient equals one (or an intraclass correlation if leave-out), so 'peer effects' in outcome-on-outcome regressions carry no behavioural content. (theoretical)
  > Without covariates, however, outcome-on-outcome peer effects are vacuous, either unity or, if the average is defined as leave-out, determined by a generic intraclass correlation coefficient.
  [nber-working-paper-series-4]
- Pairwise flow correlation of mutual funds is higher within a fund family than across families; trades within an organisation are more similar. (empirical)
  > The discussion suggests that the correlation of investor flows across units of a unique institution is higher than across independent institutions.
  [nber-working-paper-series-8]
- Social interaction intensity (local soccer games, Israel) raises retail investors' trading-volume sensitivity to past trades; no institutional or horizon evidence. (empirical)
  > Higher social interaction intensity increases the sensitivity of individual investors' trading volume and portfolio riskiness to past trades.
  [social-interaction-intensity]

### Atomic item: Q1c Ownership-base dispersion/heterogeneity

  [journal-of-financial-economics-153-2024-103795]

### Atomic item: Q1d Common ownership, overlap, fire sales, fragility
- Investors with similar portfolio structures (cosine similarity network, Infomap communities) trade more similarly than investors outside their group. (empirical)
  > investors with similar portfolio structures to a great extent trade in a similar way
  [arxiv14022494v2-q-fingn-28-aug-2014]
- Forced flow-induced trades of mutual funds in distress create temporary price pressure in commonly held stocks that reverses afterwards. (empirical)
  > Funds experiencing large outflows (inflows) tend to decrease (increase) existing positions, which creates price pressure in the securities held in common by these funds.
  [nber-working-paper-series-2]
- Ownership by large institutions predicts higher volatility, noisier prices and greater crisis fragility via granularity (correlated behaviour of subunits). (empirical)
  > ownership by large institutions predicts higher volatility and greater noise in stock prices as well as greater fragility in times of crisis
  [nber-working-paper-series-8]
- US prime MMFs reduce exposure to issuers whose other investors have similar portfolios, to avoid joint liquidation costs. (empirical)
  > MMFs consistently shift away from security issuers exposed to similar MMFs
  [similar-investors1]
- Portfolio-overlap links validated against a bipartite configuration model null rise before the 2007-08 crisis and peak at it, signalling fire-sale systemic risk. (empirical)
  > the proportion of validated links increased steadily before the 2007-2008 financial crisis and reached a maximum when the crisis occurred
  [statistically-validated-network-of-portfolio-overlaps-and-systemic-risk-pmc]
- A stock is fragile if owners are concentrated or face correlated/volatile liquidity shocks; fragility (owner-weight quadratic form on flow covariance) strongly predicts price volatility. (empirical)
  > An asset can be fragile because of concentrated ownership, or because its owners face correlated or volatile liquidity shocks, i.e., they must buy or sell at the same time.
  [stock-price-fragility-2]
- Fragility effect is smaller where arbitrageurs (hedge funds) trade against mutual-fund liquidity shocks and larger where they trade in the same direction. (empirical)
  > fragility exerts a more modest effect on volatility among stocks in which arbitrageurs are willing to trade against liquidity shocks
  [stock-price-fragility]
- Co-fragility (correlated flows of owners across stocks) forecasts return comovement. (empirical)
  > We define two assets to be 'co-fragile' if their owners have correlated liquidity needs
  [stock-price-fragility]
- Fund portfolio overlap during 2007-2009 is far larger than expected under random allocation, indicating correlated strategies. (empirical)
  > large overlap is far more likely than expected from benchmark models of random allocation of investments
  [systemic-risk-from-investment-similarities-pmc]
- In the propagation model a fund imitates the liquidations of the most similar fund with probability equal to their cosine similarity. (theoretical)
  > a fund i that reacts to a negative shock may imitate the behavior of the most similar fund j that has already reacted. This occurs with probability given by the value of their cosine similarity.
  [systemic-risk-from-investment-similarities-pmc]
- Shared active mutual fund ownership forecasts cross-sectional excess return correlation after controlling for factor exposure, style/sector similarity and analyst coverage. (empirical)
  > We connect stocks through their common active mutual fund owners. We show that the degree of shared ownership forecasts cross-sectional variation in return correlation
  [the-journal-of-finance-vol-lxix-no-3-june-2014]
- The 2003 mutual fund trading scandal provides exogenous outflow-driven variation in common ownership that forecasts correlation, supporting a causal interpretation. (empirical)
  > We argue that shared ownership causes this excess comovement based on evidence from a natural experiment
  [the-journal-of-finance-vol-lxix-no-3-june-2014]
- Socially connected (same-neighbourhood) fund managers hold and trade more similar portfolios than same-city different-neighbourhood managers. (empirical)
  > socially connected fund managers have more similar holdings and trades
  [the-people-in-your-neighborhood-social-interactions-and-mutual-fund-portfolios-n]
- Mutual funds with higher turnover and flow volatility (proxies for liquidity needs of their investor base) liquidated more corporate bonds in the crisis. (empirical)
  > high turnover / high flow-volatility mutual funds indeed liquidated greater portions of their corporate bond holdings
  [the-role-of-institutional-investors-in-propagating-the-crisis-of-2007-2008]

### Atomic item: Q1e Peer effects, herding and identification
- Aggregated flow-induced trading across all mutual funds holding a stock has a significant but temporary price impact, reversing in subsequent years. (empirical)
  > I construct a measure of demand shocks to individual stocks by aggregating flow-induced trading across all mutual funds, and document a significant, temporary price impact of such uninformed trading.
  [a-flow-based-explanation-for-return-predictability]
- In endogenous peer-group formation, stable groups must be sufficiently homogeneous in preferences, with some heterogeneity persisting as groups grow. (theoretical)
  > We characterize stable groups and find that they must be sufficiently homogeneous.
  [american-economic-journal-microeconomics-2013-53-6996]
- Danish registry: neighbours strongest peer influence on stock trading, then coworkers, then family; IV uses third-degree peers' demographics and shock sensitivity. (empirical)
  > Neighbors exert the strongest influence on trading behavior, followed by co-workers and family members.
  [balakina-and-stockler-2025-peer-effects-in-multi-layer-networks-bse-netsci]
- Leary-Roberts instrument peers' policies with peers' idiosyncratic return shocks (controls for own and industry return, placebo test); one s.d. peer leverage change associated with 11% own change. (empirical)
  > we use peer firms' idiosyncratic equity returns (i.e., return shocks) as an instrument for peer firms' financial policies.
  [do-peer-firms-aﬀect-corporate-financial-policy]
- Herding funds (following the institutional crowd) underperform antiherding funds by over 2% per year, attributed to skill differences. (empirical)
  > herding funds underperform their antiherding peers by over 2% per year.
  [does-herding-behavior-reveal-skill]
- Funds with stronger herding propensity are older and have lower portfolio turnover. (empirical)
  > funds with a stronger propensity to herd tend to be older and less active, as indicated by the lower portfolio turnover.
  [does-herding-behavior-reveal-skill]
- With averaging (DeGroot) updating, convergence to consensus is slowed by homophily and is unaffected by network density. (theoretical)
  > convergence to a consensus is slowed by the presence of homophily, but is not influenced by network density
  [how-homophily-aﬀects-the-speed-of]
- Endogenous and exogenous peer effects are generally identified under network interaction (intransitive triads let peers-of-peers' characteristics instrument peers' outcomes), though some structures fail. (theoretical)
  > We show that endogenous and exogenous effects are generally identified under network interaction, although identification may fail for some particular structures.
  [iza-dp-no-2652]
- Reflection problem: simultaneity creates perfect collinearity between expected group mean outcome and group mean characteristics. (theoretical)
  > even in the absence of correlated effects, simultaneity in behavior of interacting agents introduces a perfect collinearity between the expected mean outcome of the group and its mean characteristics.
  [iza-dp-no-2652]
- Demand-system with IV from investment-universe restrictions identifies institutional demand elasticities; used to assess institutions' role in volatility, liquidity. (empirical)
  > We propose two identification strategies for the asset demand system, based on a coefficient restriction or instrumental variables
  [nber-working-paper-series-12]
- Investors trading against constrained funds earn highly significant returns for providing liquidity; flow-driven trades are predictable enabling front-running. (empirical)
  > investors who trade against constrained mutual funds earn highly significant returns for providing liquidity when few others are willing or able
  [nber-working-paper-series-2]
- With predetermined covariates, a peer effect test is equivalent to testing 2SLS (group dummies as instruments) = OLS, which can differ for reasons unrelated to social effects (measurement error). (theoretical)
  > the question of whether peer effects or social spillovers exist is econometrically identical to that of whether a 2SLS estimator using group dummies to instrument individual characteristics differs from OLS estimates
  [nber-working-paper-series-4]
- Randomized and quasi-experimental manipulation of peer characteristics is the strongest evidence; such designs have found little socially significant peer effect. (empirical)
  > As an empirical matter, designs of this sort have uncovered little in the way of socially significant causal effects.
  [nber-working-paper-series-4]
- Exclusion bias: including selection-pool fixed effects while excluding i from own peer set creates a mechanical negative correlation between i's characteristic and peers' mean, biasing peer effect estimates downward, also with self-selected peers. (theoretical)
  > if i is above average, the average of those remaining in the pool is lower than i; conversely, if i is below average, the average of those remaining in the pool is higher than i.
  [nber-working-paper-series-5]
- They derive exact bias formulas and an estimator of endogenous and exogenous peer effects without instruments that corrects reflection and exclusion bias, at the cost of restrictions on correlated effects. (theoretical)
  > The method corrects for the presence of reflection and exclusion bias but imposes restrictions on correlated effects.
  [nber-working-paper-series-5]
- Random assignment does not eliminate the peer-outcome correlation bias; it produces a negative correlation in standard tests of random assignment with pool fixed effects. (theoretical)
  > random assignment produces a negative correlation between peer outcomes in standard tests of random assignment with selection pool fixed effects.
  [nber-working-paper-series-5]
- Coworkers' ESPP participation and quick-sale decisions influence employees; identified comparing same-firm employees in different CBSAs with metro x window and firm x window fixed effects. (empirical)
  > Comparing only employees within a firm during the same election window and controlling for a metro area fixed effect, we find that the local choices of coworkers to participate in the firm's ESPP exert a significant influence on employees' own decisions to participate.
  [nber-working-paper-series-6]
- Mutual fund managers are more likely to hold, buy or sell a stock when other managers in the same city (other fund families) do, controlling for distance to the stock and style. (empirical)
  > A mutual-fund manager is more likely to hold (or buy, or sell) a particular stock in any quarter if other managers in the same city are holding (or buying, or selling) that same stock.
  [nber-working-paper-series]
- Friends-of-friends IVs are invalid if the network is mismeasured because unobserved peers-of-peers may be direct peers; clustering makes this likely. (theoretical)
  > When the network is mismeasured, peers of peers who are not peers in the observed network can, in fact, be peers in the real network.
  [peer-effects-in-networks-a-survey]
- Approaches to correlated effects and network endogeneity: random peers, random shocks, structural endogeneity, panel data. (theoretical)
  > We classify these approaches in four broad categories: random peers, random shocks, structural endogeneity and panel data.
  [peer-effects-in-networks-a-survey]
- Sampled-network friends-of-friends instruments are generally invalid (Chandrasekhar-Lewis Prop 3.4). (theoretical)
  > instruments built from friends of friends who are not friends in the observed network are generally invalid
  [peer-effects-in-networks-a-survey]
- Reflection problem is generally solved by network interactions absent correlated effects; microfoundations are not identified. (theoretical)
  > In the absence of correlated effects, the reflection problem is generally solved by network interactions even in non-linear, heterogeneous models. By contrast, microfoundations are generally not identified.
  [peer-effects-in-networks-a-survey]
- Champagne et al. Modified Turnover compares actual weights to buy-and-hold weights to exclude mechanical price-driven changes. (empirical)
